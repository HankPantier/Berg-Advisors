You built a seven-figure e-commerce business by watching numbers closely: ad spend by channel, contribution margin by SKU, inventory turns before Q4. Then the money hits your personal or business investment account, and something strange happens. It goes into a target-date fund or a handful of tech stocks a friend mentioned, and it sits there. Untouched. For years.

That's the gap we see constantly with multi-channel sellers here in Newtown Square and across the country: obsessive attention to operating numbers, and almost none to the portfolio sitting behind them. Amazon, Shopify, and Walmart payouts get reinvested into inventory or parked in a brokerage account nobody revisits. Meanwhile the market drifts, your risk tolerance changes as the business grows, and the allocation you picked three years ago no longer matches the person or the balance sheet you have today.

## Why e-commerce owners drift into unbalanced portfolios

Most sellers didn't choose their current allocation. It chose itself. A strong Q4 payout goes into whatever fund performed well last year. Stock options or a business sale event get parked in a single position because there wasn't time to think it through during the busiest season. Three or four years later, one holding, often the one that happened to run hot, makes up 40 or 50 percent of the account.

That's concentration risk, and it behaves differently than the risk you already manage in your business. You know how to hedge against a single-channel dependency, which is why you sell on Amazon, Shopify, and Etsy instead of one platform. Nobody applies that same instinct to their investment account. A portfolio dominated by one sector, one stock, or one asset class carries the same fragility as a business that only sells on Amazon FBA. It works fine until it doesn't.

## What rebalancing actually protects

Rebalancing isn't about chasing higher returns. It's about keeping your risk exposure where you decided it should be, not where the market happens to have pushed it—which is why [a balanced portfolio isn't set-and-forget](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it-2). If you set a target of 70 percent equities and 30 percent bonds and equities have a strong two-year run, you might wake up at 85/15 without ever making a decision to take on more risk. Rebalancing brings the allocation back to your actual risk tolerance, which for a business owner with concentrated wealth already tied up in inventory and a company valuation, is usually more conservative than a typical W-2 employee's.

Think about your own balance sheet. If 60 percent of your net worth is already tied to one e-commerce brand, your investment portfolio should probably lean toward diversification and stability, not more concentrated risk in growth stocks correlated with the same consumer spending trends that drive your sales. A downturn that hurts discretionary e-commerce spending could hit your business revenue and your portfolio at the same time if the two aren't deliberately decorrelated.

## A practical review cadence for growing sellers

Most advisors suggest reviewing allocation at least annually, and more often during years with major cash events. For e-commerce owners, that usually means:

| Trigger event | Why it matters for allocation |
|---|---|
| Strong Q4 or holiday season payout | Large lump sums often go into whatever is easiest, not what fits your target allocation |
| Business valuation increase or exit conversation | Net worth concentration in the business changes how much risk the portfolio should carry |
| New state sales tax nexus or entity structure change | Often coincides with bringing on outside capital or changing personal cash flow needs |
| Annual tax planning meeting | The natural checkpoint to review both tax-efficient rebalancing and allocation drift |

Rebalancing also has tax consequences that pure investment advice tends to skip over. Selling appreciated positions in a taxable brokerage account triggers capital gains, and the timing matters. A CPA who understands your full tax picture, not just your portfolio, can time a rebalance around a lower-income year, harvest losses against gains from a business asset sale, or coordinate the trade with quarterly estimated payments so you're not caught paying a large tax bill and rebalancing costs in the same quarter.

## Building rebalancing into your business's financial rhythm

If your bookkeeping already runs on a monthly close and quarterly advisory calls, the portfolio conversation fits naturally into that same rhythm rather than requiring a separate annual scramble. A quarterly check doesn't mean trading every quarter. It means asking three questions on a fixed schedule: has your allocation drifted more than 5 percent from target, has a major business event changed your risk tolerance, and does a rebalance make sense now from a tax timing perspective or should it wait until next quarter.

Sellers who skip this rhythm tend to make one of two mistakes. They either never rebalance at all, letting concentration build for years until a downturn hurts twice as much, or they overreact to short-term market swings and trade too often, racking up tax costs for no real risk benefit. Neither extreme serves a business owner whose real job is running the company, not timing the market.

## Common Questions

**How often should I actually rebalance my portfolio?**
Most advisors recommend checking allocation at least once a year, with a trigger-based review after any major cash event like a strong holiday payout or a business valuation change. Rebalancing too frequently adds tax cost without a meaningful risk benefit, so tie it to a schedule, not to daily market moves.

**Does my e-commerce business count as part of my investment portfolio?**
Yes, in the sense that it represents concentrated risk tied to consumer spending and platform policy changes. A financial plan that only looks at your brokerage account while ignoring that your business is 60 percent of your net worth is missing the bigger risk picture entirely.

**Can rebalancing trigger a big tax bill?**
It can, since selling appreciated positions in a taxable account creates capital gains. Coordinating the timing with a lower-income year, a loss elsewhere in your business, or your quarterly estimated payments keeps the tax hit from becoming a surprise.

**Is a CPA the right person to talk to about asset allocation?**
A CPA won't manage your trades, but the tax and cash flow context we bring, especially around business income concentration and timing of gains, shapes whether a given rebalancing move actually makes financial sense for your specific situation.
