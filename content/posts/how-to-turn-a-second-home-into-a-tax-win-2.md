Plenty of Berg Advisors clients run six and seven-figure e-commerce businesses out of a home office in Newtown Square, then start eyeing a lake house in the Poconos or a place near the Jersey Shore as the business finally throws off real cash. The question that comes up almost every time is some version of: can I write this off? The honest answer is that a second home can absolutely reduce your tax bill, but the IRS decides how based on something most buyers never think about until it's too late: how many days you personally use the place versus how many days you rent it out.

Get that math wrong and you either lose deductions you were entitled to or accidentally convert your weekend retreat into a rental business with its own reporting requirements. Get it right, and a second home becomes one of the more flexible tax tools available to a profitable e-commerce owner.

## The line the IRS draws between a home and a rental

Under [IRS Tax Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415), a property you own is treated as a residence, not a rental, if your personal use during the year exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10 percent of the days it's rented at fair market value. Cross that threshold and the IRS caps how much of your rental-related expenses you can deduct, even if you rented the place out most of the year.

This is the rule people mean when they mention the "14-day rule." Rent the property for 14 days or fewer in a calendar year and the IRS doesn't consider it a rental at all: you don't report the income, and you don't deduct rental expenses either. For an e-commerce owner who wants to rent the beach house out for a couple of long weekends during peak season to offset the mortgage, that's a genuinely useful provision. Rent it for two weeks over the summer at $2,500 a week and pocket $5,000 tax-free, no Schedule E required.

Stay under that personal-use threshold and rent the property out more aggressively, and the tax treatment flips. Now it's a rental property, expenses and depreciation get deducted against rental income on Schedule E, and losses may be limited by passive activity rules described in [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527).

## Second home vs investment property: the distinction that changes everything

"Second home" and "investment property" get used interchangeably in real estate listings, but the IRS treats them very differently, and so does your mortgage lender.

| | Second home (personal use dominant) | Investment / rental property |
|---|---|---|
| Personal use limit | More than 14 days or 10% of rental days | 14 days or fewer, or below the 10% threshold |
| Mortgage interest | Deductible as a residence on Schedule A, subject to the $750,000 combined mortgage limit | Deductible as a rental expense on Schedule E |
| Rental income | Tax-free if rented 14 days or fewer | Fully taxable, reported on Schedule E |
| Expense deductions | Limited to rental income; can't create a loss | Can generate a deductible loss, subject to passive activity limits |
| Depreciation | Not allowed | Allowed, spread over 27.5 years |

The practical decision usually comes down to how you actually intend to use the place. A family that wants three weeks at the shore every summer and rents it out occasionally to cover costs is living in second-home territory. An owner who buys a duplex near Rehoboth specifically to run it as a short-term rental and visits twice a year for maintenance is running a rental business, whether they've mentally framed it that way or not.

## Why this matters more for e-commerce owners specifically

Most of the multi-channel sellers we work with already juggle revenue across Amazon, Shopify, Walmart, and eBay, reconciling deposits that hit at different times with fees and returns baked in at different rates. Adding a second property with its own income and expense stream, sometimes personal, sometimes rental depending on the month, is exactly the kind of complexity that gets misreported when it's handled on the side with a spreadsheet.

A few patterns show up repeatedly with clients in this position:

- Seasonal cash flow from Q4 e-commerce sales funds the down payment, but the mortgage and carrying costs hit year-round, which changes how much personal use versus rental use actually makes financial sense.
- Owners who already run an S-corp or LLC for their e-commerce business sometimes assume the same entity can hold the vacation property. It usually shouldn't, both for liability reasons and because it muddies the personal-use test.
- Inventory-heavy sellers who are used to accrual accounting for their business often apply the wrong mindset to the second home, trying to depreciate a property they use personally more than 14 days a year, which the IRS doesn't allow.

Andrew Berg, CPA and founder of Berg Advisors, sees this come up almost every fall as sellers head into peak season with extra cash on hand. "The clients who get the most value out of a second home are the ones who decide the usage pattern before they close, not after the first tax season," he says. "Fourteen days is a hard line. Fifteen days of personal use in a year and the tax treatment changes completely, so it's worth mapping out the calendar before you sign anything."

## Running the numbers on a real scenario

Say a Shopify and Amazon seller based in Delaware County buys a $450,000 property in the Poconos with a $360,000 mortgage. They plan to use it 20 days a year themselves and rent it out 60 days during ski season and summer weekends at $300 a night.

Because personal use (20 days) exceeds 14 days and also exceeds 10 percent of rental days (6 days), this property is a residence with limited rental use, not a full rental. That means:

- Rental income of roughly $18,000 (60 nights at $300) is reportable on Schedule E.
- Rental expenses, cleaning, property management, a portion of utilities and mortgage interest, are deductible, but only up to the amount of rental income. No loss can be claimed.
- Mortgage interest and property taxes attributable to personal-use days can still be deducted on Schedule A as a second home, subject to the usual limits.
- Depreciation isn't available because personal use exceeds the threshold.

Drop personal use to 10 days instead of 20 and rent it out 90 nights, and the calculus flips. The property now qualifies as a rental. Depreciation becomes available, losses can offset other income subject to passive activity rules, and the reporting gets meaningfully more complex, but so does the tax benefit.

Neither answer is automatically better. It depends on whether the owner wants a place to actually use, a cash-flowing asset, or some blend of both. That's a conversation worth having with a CPA before closing, not after the first 1099 shows up from a booking platform.

## Getting the paperwork right from day one

Once the usage pattern is decided, the bookkeeping matters just as much as the initial classification. Platforms like Airbnb and Vrbo issue 1099-Ks once you cross reporting thresholds, and that income needs to reconcile against actual bank deposits the same way Amazon and Shopify payouts do for the e-commerce side of the business. Owners who already track multi-channel revenue with a proactive bookkeeping process tend to fold a second property in without much friction. Owners who've been managing books reactively usually find the second property is what finally forces a better system.

## Common Questions

**How many days can I rent out my second home without paying taxes on the income?**
Under the IRS 14-day rule described in [Tax Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415), you can rent a property for 14 days or fewer in a calendar year and skip reporting that income entirely, as long as you also use it personally. Rent it for a 15th day and all the rental income becomes reportable.

**Is a lake house a second home or an investment property for tax purposes?**
It depends on personal use, not on what the mortgage paperwork calls it. If you use it more than 14 days a year or more than 10 percent of the days it's rented, the IRS treats it as a residence with limited rental activity rather than an investment property, which caps your deductible expenses at your rental income.

**Can I deduct a loss on my second home if I rent it out part of the year?**
Only if the property qualifies as a rental under the personal-use test. If personal use exceeds 14 days or 10 percent of rental days, expenses are limited to rental income and no loss can be claimed. Cross below that threshold and losses may be deductible subject to passive activity rules in [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527).

**Does owning an e-commerce business change how a second home is taxed?**
Not directly, but it changes the practical stakes. Sellers already managing multi-channel revenue and inventory accounting tend to have more complex tax returns overall, and adding a second property with its own income stream raises the cost of getting the classification wrong.
