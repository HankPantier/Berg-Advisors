A seller doing $2.5M across Amazon, Shopify, and Walmart FBA reached out to us last spring convinced he was underinsured. He'd just read a horror story about a warehouse fire wiping out a competitor's inventory. What we found instead was the opposite problem: he was paying for a general liability rider covering a storefront he didn't have, an umbrella policy sized for a net worth he'd left behind two years ago, and still had zero coverage for the $180,000 in FBA inventory sitting in Amazon's fulfillment centers. He wasn't underinsured. He was insured in all the wrong places.

That's the pattern we see most often with multi-channel e-commerce clients. Insurance gets bought reactively, one policy at a time, usually after a scare or a marketplace requirement, never against an actual picture of what you're protecting. The fix isn't a longer checklist of policies. It's a risk-based calculation tied to your net worth, your inventory exposure, and your cash flow, run the same way you'd model a tax position.

## Start with what you're actually protecting, not what's available to buy

Insurance exists to transfer risk you can't afford to absorb yourself. That means the starting point isn't a list of product types, it's your balance sheet. Add up liquid net worth, inventory value at cost (not retail), outstanding business debt, and any personal guarantees you've signed for suppliers or a line of credit. That number is your real exposure ceiling.

A seller with $150,000 in liquid savings and $300,000 in inventory has a very different risk profile than one with $1.2 million in savings and the same inventory load. The first seller needs coverage that protects operating continuity. The second can likely self-insure smaller losses and reserve premium dollars for the catastrophic scenarios, a total warehouse loss, a product liability suit, a cyberattack that locks up customer payment data.

We run this exercise with clients as part of quarterly advisory conversations, not as a one-time event. Net worth and inventory value both move throughout the year, especially heading into Q4, so a coverage plan set in January can be badly out of date by Black Friday.

## The coverage gaps specific to multi-channel sellers

Generic small business insurance guides assume a storefront or an office. Multi-channel sellers carry a different set of exposures:

| Risk area | Why generic advice misses it |
|---|---|
| Inventory in FBA warehouses | Amazon's reimbursement policy covers loss/damage at limited, often below-cost rates, not full replacement value |
| Product liability across platforms | A defect claim from an Etsy buyer and a Walmart Marketplace buyer can trigger separate insurer notification requirements |
| Cyber and data breach | Standard general liability policies typically exclude data breach costs entirely |
| Multi-state exposure | Nexus in a state can trigger workers' comp or business registration insurance requirements you didn't know applied |
| Owner disability | Sellers who are the sole operator of the brand rarely carry income-replacement coverage sized to actual draws |

The inventory gap is the one we flag most. Amazon's own reimbursement terms cap payouts well below what it costs you to replace lost or damaged units, and separate contingent business interruption coverage is often the only way to close that gap. If your cost of goods sold on FBA inventory runs $400,000 at any given time, that's the number your policy needs to reference, not a round figure your broker suggested three years ago.

## Business insurance coverage checklist, reframed as a decision tree

Rather than working through a checklist of every available policy type, work through three questions in order:

1. **What loss would force me to shut down or take on debt within 90 days?** That's the coverage you buy first, regardless of cost. For most multi-channel sellers, that's inventory and general liability.
2. **What loss would be painful but survivable using cash reserves?** That's where a higher deductible often makes more sense than a lower-premium, lower-deductible policy. If you're carrying $200,000 in operating cash, you may not need first-dollar coverage on smaller claims.
3. **What loss is remote but catastrophic if it happens?** Umbrella liability and cyber coverage usually land here. Premiums are relatively low against the exposure they cover, which is exactly why they're worth carrying even when cash flow is tight.

This is a personal risk management planning exercise as much as a business one if you're structured as an LLC or S-corp and your personal assets sit close to the business. A supplier lawsuit or a customer injury claim that pierces a thin corporate veil doesn't stay a business problem for long.

## Types of insurance for financial protection, sized correctly

Once the decision tree tells you where your real exposure sits, matching it to a policy type gets simpler:

- **General liability**: sized to your revenue and claim history, not a flat industry-standard number
- **Product liability**: essential if you manufacture or private-label, less critical if you're strictly a reseller of established branded goods
- **Inventory/property coverage**: sized to replacement cost across every location, including third-party fulfillment centers
- **Cyber liability**: sized to the customer records you store, particularly if you handle payment data outside a processor like Stripe or Shopify Payments
- **Umbrella policy**: sized to push total liability coverage above your net worth, so a judgment can't reach your house or investment accounts
- **Key person / disability**: sized to what it would cost to replace your operational role for 12 months, not a generic multiple of salary

Insurance premiums for a legitimate business risk are generally deductible as an ordinary business expense, per [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535), which is one more reason to size coverage deliberately rather than either overbuying out of anxiety or underbuying to save on premium.

## Why this belongs in a quarterly conversation, not an annual renewal

Most sellers we work with review insurance once a year, right before a renewal notice arrives, and treat it as a rubber-stamp exercise. That's backwards for a business where inventory value, revenue, and marketplace mix can shift by 30% or more between Q1 and Q4. We build insurance review into the same quarterly advisory calls where we look at cash flow and tax planning, because the inputs are the same numbers. A seller who just added a Walmart Marketplace channel or moved inventory into a second 3PL warehouse has a different risk profile than they did six months earlier, and their coverage should move with it.

The same logic applies to other big financial decisions that hinge on your actual numbers rather than a generic rule of thumb. If you're weighing [buying versus renting a home](/resources/do-the-math-behind-renting-vs-buying-a-home) against seasonal e-commerce income, or trying to protect against [medical expenses that could otherwise wreck a good quarter](/resources/why-tax-advantaged-planning-is-the-real-defense-against-medi), the calculation looks a lot like the one above: start with the real exposure, then size the tool to match it.

## Common Questions

**How much insurance coverage do you need as an e-commerce seller?**
Enough to cover any loss that would force you into debt or shut the business down within 90 days, plus catastrophic coverage (umbrella, product liability) sized above your total net worth. For a seller with $300,000 in inventory and $150,000 in liquid savings, that typically means full replacement-cost inventory coverage plus a $1-2 million umbrella policy.

**Does Amazon's FBA reimbursement policy count as inventory insurance?**
No. Amazon reimburses lost or damaged inventory at rates that often fall below your actual cost, and claims are frequently disputed or delayed. Sellers with significant FBA inventory typically need separate contingent business interruption or inventory coverage to close that gap.

**Should I buy separate cyber liability coverage if I use Shopify Payments or Stripe?**
Using a compliant processor reduces your exposure but doesn't eliminate it. You're still liable for a breach of customer names, addresses, and order history stored in your own systems, email platform, or CRM. A standalone cyber policy is usually inexpensive relative to that exposure.

**How often should I reassess my business insurance coverage?**
Quarterly, alongside a review of inventory value, revenue, and net worth. A policy sized correctly in January can leave real gaps by Q4 if your inventory load or channel mix has shifted significantly.
