A client running a home-goods brand across Amazon, Shopify, and Walmart asked us this spring whether the German Shepherd guarding her overflow inventory in a Newtown Square warehouse counted as a write-off. Her golden retriever at home, unfortunately, does not. That distinction trips up more business owners than you'd think, especially multi-channel sellers who store inventory outside their homes and genuinely wonder where the line sits.

The short answer: your dog is a deduction only if it's actually working for the business, not just keeping you company while you pack orders. The IRS doesn't care how much the dog helps your mood during a brutal Q4. It cares whether the expense is ordinary and necessary for producing income, per [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535). Everything below comes down to that one test.

## Why the family dog doesn't make the cut

Publication 535 sets the bar for any business expense: it has to be ordinary in your trade and necessary to run it. A pet that lives in your house, sleeps on your couch, and occasionally sits next to you on Zoom calls fails that test even if you post it on your brand's Instagram once a month. The IRS treats that as a personal expense dressed up as a marketing line item, and it's one of the first things an examiner flags on a Schedule C with inflated advertising or supplies costs.

We see this most with e-commerce founders who work from home and want to fold the dog's food, vet bills, and grooming into business expenses because the dog appears in product photos or unboxing videos. Occasional appearances don't establish business use. A recurring, documented role does.

## Guard dogs protecting inventory are a different conversation

If you store inventory in a warehouse, storage unit, or dedicated fulfillment space, a dog kept there specifically for security is a legitimate business expense. This comes up constantly for our multi-channel e-commerce clients who outgrow home storage and lease space to hold pallets of product between Amazon FBA shipments. A guard dog housed at that location, trained or suited to that role, and not living in your home, clears the ordinary-and-necessary test the same way an alarm system or security camera would.

What you can typically deduct in that scenario:

- Food and veterinary care while the dog is stationed at the business location
- Training costs specific to guard or protection work
- A reasonable portion of the dog's purchase price, depreciated as a business asset in some cases

What won't survive an audit: claiming 100% of expenses for a dog that also lives at your house on weekends, or a breed with no protective function that happens to bark at strangers. Andrew Berg, CPA, works with inventory-heavy sellers on exactly this kind of outsourced CFO question, and his standard advice is simple: if the dog's business role can't be explained in one sentence to an IRS agent, it's not documented well enough yet.

## Service and guide dogs are a medical deduction, not a business one

This is where a lot of confusion sets in. A service or guide dog trained to assist someone with a disability, including visual impairment, hearing loss, or certain mobility and psychiatric conditions, is deductible, but as a medical expense on Schedule A, not a business expense. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) covers the costs of buying, training, and maintaining the animal, including food and veterinary care, as part of your medical deduction, subject to the usual 7.5% adjusted gross income floor.

That distinction matters for business owners who assume a service animal automatically becomes a Schedule C write-off because they run their business from home. It doesn't work that way. The deduction follows the medical purpose, not the location where the dog happens to sit during business hours.

## Therapy dogs, breeding operations, and brand mascots

Three scenarios come up often enough to spell out separately.

**Therapy dogs.** A dog that provides general comfort or emotional support, without formal training tied to a diagnosed condition and a treatment plan from a licensed provider, generally doesn't qualify for either the medical deduction under Publication 502 or a business deduction under Publication 535. "He calms me down before big sales pushes" is a true statement and not a tax position.

**Dog breeding as a business.** If you're running an actual breeding operation with a profit motive, keeping records, advertising litters, and reporting income, ordinary business expense rules apply just like any other Schedule C activity. The catch is the hobby-loss rules: the IRS expects to see real business behavior, not a few litters a year alongside a full-time e-commerce job with no separate books.

**Brand mascot dogs.** If your dog is a genuine, ongoing part of your marketing, appearing in a set percentage of product photography, running its own tagged social account that drives traffic, or featured in paid ad creative, a portion of related costs can sometimes be allocated as an advertising expense. The word that matters is portion. You're not deducting the dog's entire existence because it occasionally appears in a listing photo.

## A quick reference for e-commerce sellers

| Scenario | Deduction type | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| Guard dog at a warehouse or fulfillment space | Business expense | Schedule C |
| Service or guide dog for a diagnosed condition | Medical expense | Schedule A, Publication 502 |
| Family pet featured occasionally in social posts | Not deductible | N/A |
| Legitimate breeding operation with profit motive | Business expense | Schedule C |
| Brand mascot with a consistent, documented marketing role | Partial advertising expense | Schedule C |
| Emotional support dog without a treatment plan | Not deductible | N/A |

## Documentation is what separates a deduction from an audit flag

Every legitimate case above shares one requirement: paperwork that would make sense to a stranger. That means a written business purpose, receipts tied to the business location or medical provider, and a percentage-of-use calculation you can defend rather than estimate on the spot. Michael Laudazio, who works with our multi-entity and multi-state clients on compliance questions, points out that dog-related deductions rarely trigger an audit on their own, but they're an easy line item for an examiner to disallow quickly if there's no supporting record, which then invites a closer look at everything else on the return.

For sellers running inventory across multiple states and platforms, that closer look is the last thing you want. If you're already juggling multi-state sales tax nexus on top of daily operations, a shaky $400 guard dog deduction isn't worth the exposure.

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct my dog if it appears in my Amazon or Shopify product photos?**
Occasional appearances don't qualify. If the dog has a consistent, documented marketing role, such as running a branded account that drives measurable traffic, you may be able to deduct a reasonable portion of related costs as advertising under [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535), but not the full cost of owning the dog.

**Is a guard dog at my warehouse fully deductible?**
Generally yes, if the dog is kept at the business location specifically for security and doesn't also live at your home. Deductible costs typically include food, veterinary care, and training while stationed there. Mixed personal and business use requires an allocation, not a full deduction.

**Does a service dog count as a business expense if I work from home?**
No. Service and guide dogs are deducted as a medical expense under [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502), subject to the 7.5% adjusted gross income floor on Schedule A, regardless of where you work. The deduction follows the medical need, not your business address.

**What records do I need to support a dog-related deduction?**
Keep a written statement of business purpose, receipts for food, vet care, and training, and a log or photos showing the dog's role at the business location. For medical deductions, keep documentation from the prescribing provider along with training records for the animal.
