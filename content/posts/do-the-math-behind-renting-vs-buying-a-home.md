You've got a good Q4 behind you, a healthy cash cushion, and a Zillow tab open for a colonial in Newtown Square. The instinct is to ask, "Is now a good time to buy?" That's the wrong question. Mortgage rates move, home prices move, and nobody, including your realtor, can time either one reliably. The better question is whether buying makes sense on your actual financial statements, given how your e-commerce income actually behaves month to month. That's a math problem, not a market-timing problem, and it's one we walk through with clients constantly.

Multi-channel sellers have a specific version of this decision. Revenue from Amazon, Shopify, and Walmart doesn't arrive evenly. Q4 might carry 40% of your annual profit, and January can look bleak in comparison. Lenders don't underwrite that way, and a mortgage payment doesn't care that your best month was November. Before you fall for a listing, [run the numbers](/resources/do-the-math-behind-renting-vs-buying-a-home-2) the way you'd evaluate a large inventory buy: what does this commit you to, and what does it cost you to hold that commitment for the next several years.

## Treat this like a capital allocation decision, not a lifestyle one

A home is an illiquid, leveraged asset that also happens to be where you sleep. Strip the emotion out for a minute and it looks like any other large capital decision your business makes. You're weighing a large upfront outlay against ongoing carrying costs, comparing it to the return you'd get putting that same capital elsewhere, and factoring in how predictable your income needs to be to service the debt.

We ask e-commerce clients the same three questions we'd ask about a six-figure inventory purchase. What's the true monthly carrying cost, not just the headline mortgage payment? What's the opportunity cost of the cash tied up in the down payment? And how does this affect your ability to weather a slow quarter without touching a line of credit? Renting fails this test in one direction, tying up nothing but also building nothing. Buying fails it in the other direction if the math doesn't account for how lumpy your revenue really is.

## Running the numbers on a Newtown Square home

Here's a real-world scenario we use with clients: a $650,000 home in Newtown Square, 20% down, a 30-year fixed mortgage around 6.75%, compared against a comparable rental at $3,200 a month.

| Monthly cost | Renting | Buying |
|---|---|---|
| Rent / mortgage principal & interest | $3,200 | $3,373 |
| Property tax (Delaware County, ~1.9%) | $0 | $1,029 |
| Homeowners / renters insurance | $25 | $150 |
| Maintenance reserve (1% of home value annually) | $0 | $542 |
| **Total monthly cash outlay** | **$3,225** | **$5,094** |

Buying costs roughly $1,870 more per month in this example, before you count the $130,000 down payment or the $13,000 to $19,500 in closing costs most buyers underestimate. None of that means renting wins outright. It means the $1,870 monthly gap and the six-figure down payment need to earn their keep somewhere, either in home appreciation, in forced equity you wouldn't otherwise save, or in stability that lets you sleep better during a slow February.

## Irregular e-commerce income changes what a lender sees

Self-employed and business-owner applicants already get more underwriting scrutiny than W-2 borrowers, and multi-channel sellers add another layer. Lenders typically average two years of net business income from your tax returns, which means last year's slow start still drags down what you qualify for even if this year is your best one yet. If you run your business on a cash basis and your books mix personal and business transactions, that averaging gets messier and your approved amount can come in lower than your actual cash flow would suggest.

This is where clean books do double duty. A lender who can see clear, accrual-adjusted financials trusts the number faster than one working from a shoebox of 1099s and platform payout reports. If you're planning to apply for a mortgage in the next 12 to 18 months, get your bookkeeping current now, not the month before you submit an application.

## Your down payment competes with your investment portfolio

A $130,000 down payment sitting in a brokerage account earning a conservative 7% average annual return generates roughly $9,100 a year, or about $758 a month, in opportunity cost once it's converted into home equity instead. That's real money, and it's the piece most buy-vs-rent calculators skip entirely because they only compare monthly payments, not what the capital would otherwise be doing.

Home equity isn't liquid and doesn't pay dividends, but it isn't nothing either. It's forced savings that most people wouldn't otherwise stick to, and it comes with a place to live attached. Whether that trade beats keeping the capital invested depends on your time horizon, your risk tolerance, and how the rest of your portfolio is allocated. We've written before about why a [balanced investment portfolio](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it-2) needs active management rather than a set-it-and-forget approach, and the same logic applies here: a home purchase is a portfolio decision, not a separate category of spending.

## The mortgage interest deduction rarely moves the needle you expect

Clients often assume the mortgage interest deduction tips the math meaningfully toward buying. For most sellers taking the standard deduction, which nearly doubled under the 2017 tax law, it doesn't. You only benefit from itemizing mortgage interest if your total itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction, and on a $520,000 loan in year one, you're paying roughly $34,700 in interest, which may or may not clear that bar depending on your state and local tax deductions. [IRS Topic No. 505](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc505) covers how the deduction is calculated and which loans qualify. Run this specific to your tax bracket and filing status before you count it as a deciding factor.

The Federal Reserve's [Survey of Consumer Finances](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf20.pdf) shows a substantial gap between the median net worth of homeowners and renters, but the survey doesn't isolate how much of that gap comes from home equity versus other saving and investing behavior. Owning correlates with higher net worth. It doesn't prove that buying a specific home at a specific price beats investing the same capital and renting. That's the calculation worth doing before you sign an offer letter, not after.

## Common Questions

**Is renting actually better than buying, financially?**
It depends on how long you'll stay and what the freed-up capital does elsewhere. In the Newtown Square scenario above, buying costs about $1,870 more per month plus the opportunity cost on a $130,000 down payment. If you'd stay under five years or your income is uneven quarter to quarter, renting often wins on pure numbers, though it builds no equity.

**How much e-commerce income history do lenders want to see?**
Most conventional lenders average two years of net income from your tax returns, so a strong current year doesn't fully offset a weaker prior one. Clean, accrual-adjusted financials help lenders trust your numbers faster and can support a more accurate approval amount than raw platform payout reports.

**Should I use profits for a down payment or reinvest in the business?**
Run both as return-on-capital questions. If reinvesting in inventory or ad spend reliably returns more than 7-8% annually, that capital may work harder in the business than as home equity. If your growth has plateaued or you want diversification outside the business, a down payment starts to make more sense.

**Does the mortgage interest deduction change the math significantly?**
Usually not as much as people expect. Since the standard deduction nearly doubled in 2017, many homeowners no longer benefit from itemizing mortgage interest unless their state, local, and other deductions push them past that threshold. Check [IRS Topic No. 505](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc505) and run your specific numbers before counting it as a deciding factor.
