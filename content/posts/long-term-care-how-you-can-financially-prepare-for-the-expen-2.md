If you sell across Amazon, Shopify, and three other channels, you already spend your days pricing risk: shipping delays, chargebacks, a supplier who ghosts you mid-season. Long-term care is the same kind of risk, just decades out and dressed up as a family issue instead of a business one. Most owners we talk to have never priced it, and the number surprises them every time.

Here's the uncomfortable math: a private room in a Pennsylvania nursing home now runs $130,000 to $145,000 a year in the Philadelphia suburbs, including Newtown Square. Assisted living averages $60,000 to $70,000 annually in the region, and a full-time home health aide can cost $65,000 or more a year once you factor in overtime and holiday rates. Medicare covers almost none of it. Multiply that by an average stay of two to three years, and you're looking at a six-figure liability that most families fund with panic instead of a plan.

For e-commerce owners, this hits differently than it does for a salaried employee. Your net worth is often tied up in inventory, a business bank account, and maybe a rental property or two, not a steady paycheck with employer-sponsored long-term care insurance sitting behind it. That means the funding decision has to be made deliberately, years before anyone needs care, and it has to work alongside the retirement and estate plan you're already building.

## Why the price tag catches multi-channel sellers off guard

Most self-employed business owners underestimate long-term care costs for two reasons. First, they're comparing the expense to a hospital stay, which is capped and largely insured. Long-term care isn't capped, and it isn't a medical event covered by standard health insurance. Second, they assume Medicaid will step in. Medicaid does pay for long-term care, but only after you've spent down most of your countable assets, and for a business owner that spend-down can mean liquidating the inventory, equipment, or investment accounts that fund your retirement or your kids' inheritance.

The IRS does allow certain long-term care expenses and qualified long-term care insurance premiums to count as deductible medical expenses, subject to the usual 7.5% of adjusted gross income floor. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) lays out exactly which costs qualify, which matters more than people expect once a parent or spouse needs paid care and the bills start arriving monthly.

## Three ways to fund the cost, and what each one really means

There's no single right answer here. The right mix depends on your age, your health, your business cash flow, and whether you or a spouse served in the military. Here's how the three main paths compare.

| Funding path | How it works | Best fit | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term care insurance | You pay premiums for years; the policy pays a daily or monthly benefit if you need care | Buyers in their 50s with clean health histories and steady income | Premiums can rise over time, and underwriting gets harder after 60 |
| Self-funding | You set aside investments or business proceeds specifically earmarked for care costs | Owners with $2 million or more in investable assets outside the business | Requires discipline to keep the money untouched and growing |
| VA Aid and Attendance | A monthly benefit paid to qualifying wartime veterans and surviving spouses to help cover in-home or facility care | Veterans and spouses who meet the service and income requirements | The application process is slow, so early filing matters |

Long-term care insurance versus self-funding isn't really an either-or question for most of our clients. A policy with a $150,000 to $200,000 lifetime benefit, paired with a dedicated investment account, spreads the risk without requiring you to set aside the full six-figure cost in cash. If your business throws off strong distributions, we often build the self-funding piece into the same rebalancing conversation we have about your broader portfolio, which is a topic we cover in more detail in [our piece on why a balanced investment portfolio isn't set-and-forget](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it-2).

For veterans, the VA Aid and Attendance benefit is one of the most underused resources in long-term care planning. It's a needs-based monthly payment on top of a standard VA pension, meant to help cover the cost of an aide, assisted living, or a nursing home, and eligible spouses of veterans can qualify too. Full details on eligibility and the application process are on the [VA's Aid and Attendance benefits page](https://www.va.gov/pension/aid-attendance-housebound/). We've seen this benefit cover $2,000 to $2,700 a month for a married veteran, which meaningfully changes the self-funding math.

## Where this connects to your business and your balance sheet

Here's the part that's specific to running an e-commerce business rather than drawing a W-2 paycheck. Your liquidity is lumpy. Q4 might carry your whole year, and your working capital is tied up in inventory sitting in Amazon warehouses or a 3PL. That's exactly why long-term care planning has to be a line item in your annual advisory conversation, not an afterthought triggered by a parent's diagnosis.

A few places this shows up in practice:

- **Entity structure and cash flow.** If you're an S-corp owner taking a reasonable salary plus distributions, we can model how much of your annual distribution should route into a dedicated long-term care fund versus growth investments, without starving the business of working capital.
- **HSA dollars.** If you're on a high-deductible health plan, HSA funds can pay for a portion of qualified long-term care expenses tax-free, which pairs with the broader tax-advantaged planning we outline in [our post on defending against medical debt](/resources/why-tax-advantaged-planning-is-the-real-defense-against-medi-2).
- **Insurance coordination.** Long-term care insurance is a different animal from the business and personal liability coverage most sellers already carry. We talk through how the two fit together in [our risk-based approach to insurance coverage](/resources/insuring-where-it-counts-avoid-over-insuring-in-the-wrong-pl).
- **Aging parents, not just yourself.** A surprising number of our e-commerce clients are funding a parent's care right now while also trying to save for their own. Those two goals compete for the same dollars, and the plan has to account for both.

Andrew Berg, CPA and one of our firm's principals, spends a good part of his outsourced CFO work with product-based business owners walking through exactly this tradeoff: how much of the business's retained earnings should stay in the business, how much should move to personal investment accounts, and how much should be earmarked specifically for a parent's or spouse's future care—conversations that often surface how [burnout and exhaustion erode the judgment](/resources/burnout-isnt-a-business-strategy-self-care-should-be-part-of) needed to make these decisions well. There's no formula that fits every household, but there is a repeatable process, and running it early beats running it during a crisis every time.

## Building the plan before you need it

The single biggest mistake we see is waiting. Long-term care insurance underwriting gets stricter and more expensive after 60, VA benefit applications can take months to process, and self-funded accounts need years of growth to reach six figures without disrupting your business's cash flow. A 45-year-old e-commerce owner who starts setting aside $300 to $500 a month today, paired with even a modest insurance policy, is in a fundamentally different position at 75 than someone who starts thinking about it after a diagnosis.

Start with three questions: What would a two-year stay in a Pennsylvania facility cost your family at today's rates? How much of that could a modest insurance policy or VA benefit cover? And how much would you need to self-fund the rest without touching the business? Once you have real numbers instead of a vague sense of dread, the plan gets a lot easier to build.

## Common Questions

**Is long-term care insurance worth it if I'm self-employed?**
For most self-employed owners between 50 and 65 with clean health histories, a policy with a $150,000 to $200,000 lifetime benefit is worth pricing out, especially since premiums are often deductible as a business expense within IRS limits. It reduces how much you need to self-fund and locks in coverage before underwriting gets harder with age.

**Can VA Aid and Attendance be combined with long-term care insurance?**
Yes. The VA benefit doesn't reduce or offset a private insurance payout, so a qualifying veteran or surviving spouse can collect both. Combining them often covers a meaningful share of assisted living or in-home care costs without touching retirement savings.

**How much should an e-commerce business owner set aside for a parent's long-term care?**
There's no universal number, but families funding a parent's care for two to three years should plan for $100,000 to $250,000 in Pennsylvania, depending on the level of care needed. We build this into a broader cash flow model that accounts for the business's seasonality rather than treating it as a one-time expense.

**Does Medicaid cover long-term care if I run out of money?**
Medicaid does cover long-term care, but only after countable assets are spent down to a low threshold, which varies by state. For business owners, that spend-down can force liquidation of investment accounts or inventory-linked assets, which is exactly why proactive insurance or self-funding planning matters.
