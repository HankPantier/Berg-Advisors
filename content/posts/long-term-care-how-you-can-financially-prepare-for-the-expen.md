Most multi-channel sellers we work with have a strange balance sheet. Six or seven figures might be sitting in Amazon and Shopify inventory, a warehouse lease, or a 3PL relationship, and almost none of it is liquid. That works fine until a parent has a fall, a stroke, or a dementia diagnosis, and suddenly the family needs $8,000 to $12,000 a month for care. If your net worth is tied up in pallets and product SKUs, long-term care is one of the few expenses that can force a fire sale of assets you spent a decade building.

Long-term care planning isn't a topic people like to sit with. It touches aging, mortality, and family dynamics all at once. But it's also a straightforward risk-management problem, the same kind you already solve when you buy inventory insurance or diversify sales channels so one platform suspension doesn't sink the business. The difference is timing: [long-term care has to be funded years before anyone needs it](/resources/long-term-care-how-you-can-financially-prepare-for-the-expen-2), not after the diagnosis arrives.

## The price tag most families underestimate

A private room in a skilled nursing facility now commonly runs $10,000 to $12,000 a month in the Philadelphia suburbs, and home health aides typically bill $30 to $35 an hour, which adds up fast once care moves from a few hours a day to round-the-clock support. Assisted living communities in Delaware County and Montgomery County generally land somewhere between those two figures. None of this is a one-time cost. Care can last two years or fifteen, and the average stay tends toward the longer end for anyone with dementia.

Medicare does not cover custodial long-term care. It pays for short-term rehab after a hospital stay, up to roughly 100 days, and that's it. Families who assume Medicare will step in usually find out the truth during the second week of a nursing home stay, when the bill arrives.

## Why e-commerce owners carry extra risk here

If you're a W-2 employee with a diversified 401(k), a long-term care event is painful but the funding math is at least simple: liquidate some investments, tap savings, adjust the budget. If your wealth is concentrated in a single business, especially one where working capital is locked up in inventory ahead of Q4, an unplanned $150,000 to $300,000 care expense creates a different kind of problem. You either pull cash out of the business at the worst possible time, sell inventory at a discount to generate liquidity, or take on debt against a company that was never built to absorb a personal financial shock.

This is also a two-generation issue. Adult children running e-commerce businesses are frequently the ones fielding the call when a parent needs care, and they're the ones deciding whether to self-fund, file a VA claim, or start a Medicaid spend-down conversation with an elder law attorney. Planning ahead protects both generations' balance sheets, not just the person receiving care.

## Three ways to fund long-term care

There isn't one right answer here. The right mix depends on age, health, existing assets, and whether a parent or spouse served in the military. Most families end up using some combination of the three paths below.

| Funding path | How it works | Best fit |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term care insurance | A policy purchased in your 50s or early 60s pays a daily or monthly benefit toward care costs, usually for a defined number of years | Healthy applicants who want to cap their exposure and preserve business and investment assets |
| Self-funding | Savings, brokerage accounts, or a dedicated long-term care reserve cover costs directly as they come due | Higher net worth households, or those who were declined coverage or find premiums too costly |
| VA Aid and Attendance benefit | A monthly, tax-free pension benefit for wartime veterans and surviving spouses who need help with daily activities | Veterans and widows/widowers of veterans who meet service and income requirements |

Long-term care insurance premiums rise with age and health status, so a policy bought at 55 can cost half of what the same coverage costs at 65. The tradeoff is you're paying for coverage you may never use, and premiums aren't guaranteed to stay flat over 20 or 30 years. Self-funding avoids that premium risk entirely but requires real discipline: setting aside a dedicated account, separate from retirement and business reserves, that's earmarked for care and left alone.

A lot of families do a hybrid: a modest insurance policy to cover the first few years of care, backed by savings for anything beyond that, with a plan to apply for VA benefits if there's military service in the picture. That combination spreads the risk instead of betting the whole outcome on one funding source.

## The VA Aid and Attendance benefit few families claim

The [VA Aid and Attendance and Housebound benefit](https://www.va.gov/pension/aid-attendance-housebound/) is one of the more underused resources in this space. It's an increased monthly pension amount available to eligible wartime veterans, and to surviving spouses, who need help with daily activities like bathing, dressing, or managing medications, or who are largely confined to their home. Unlike VA disability compensation, it isn't tied to a service-connected condition. It's based on need and income, and the benefit can be applied directly toward the cost of a home health aide, assisted living, or a nursing facility.

The application process asks for medical documentation and a clear accounting of income and unreimbursed medical expenses, which is where a lot of families get stuck. If a parent or grandparent served during a recognized wartime period, it's worth running the numbers before assuming self-funding is the only option.

Some long-term care costs also qualify as deductible medical expenses once they cross the threshold described in [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502), which covers medical and dental expenses including long-term care services. That deduction won't offset a $10,000-a-month bill on its own, but it's part of the full financial picture, and it's easy to miss if nobody is tracking it against your other itemized deductions.

## Building long-term care into your broader financial plan

This is where the planning conversation usually connects back to the rest of your financial life, not just the care decision itself. If you're rebalancing a portfolio for retirement, a dedicated long-term care reserve should be part of that allocation, not an afterthought bolted on later. We've written before about why a [balanced investment portfolio](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it-2) needs regular rebalancing rather than a set-it-and-forget approach, and long-term care funding is one of the clearest reasons why: the money needs to be there and liquid when it's needed, not locked into an illiquid position.

## Common Questions

**Is long-term care insurance worth it if I'm already in my 60s?**
It can be, but premiums rise sharply with age and existing health conditions can lead to denial or exclusions. Most advisors recommend applying between 50 and 65 while you're still insurable at a reasonable rate. Past that window, self-funding or a hybrid life insurance policy with a long-term care rider often makes more financial sense.

**Can Medicaid pay for nursing home care?**
Yes, but only after you've spent down most non-exempt assets to your state's eligibility threshold, and Pennsylvania applies a five-year lookback period on asset transfers. Families who want to protect assets while planning for potential Medicaid eligibility usually need to start structuring accounts and trusts years in advance with an elder law attorney.

**What's the difference between VA Aid and Attendance and standard VA pension?**
Standard VA pension is a base monthly benefit for low-income wartime veterans over 65 or with a disability. Aid and Attendance adds an increased monthly amount on top of that base pension for veterans or surviving spouses who need help with daily living activities or are housebound, and it can be applied directly toward care costs.

**Should my long-term care reserve be separate from my business emergency fund?**
Yes. Business working capital needs to stay available for inventory, payroll, and platform fee timing, and mixing it with a personal care reserve creates pressure to raid one for the other. Keeping them in separate accounts, sized against separate goals, protects both the business and the family member who eventually needs care.
