You sponsor a Saturday 5K for the local food bank, drive two hours round trip to drop off donated inventory at a Delaware County shelter, and spend a weekend at a nonprofit board retreat instead of managing your Amazon storefront. None of that shows up on your P&L, and a lot of e-commerce owners assume none of it belongs on their tax return either. Some assume the opposite: that the 40 hours a month they give to a nonprofit board is worth something to the IRS. Both assumptions are wrong, and the gap between them is where real deductions get missed every filing season.

The rule is simpler than most people expect once you separate two things: your time, which the IRS will never value, and your out-of-pocket costs, which it often will.

## Your time has value, just not to the IRS

If you spend 15 hours designing a new website for a 501(c)(3) or you're a CPA who preps a nonprofit's books for free, you cannot deduct what that time would normally bill at. It doesn't matter if your hourly rate is $50 or $500. IRS Publication 526 is direct about this: services donated to a qualified organization are not deductible, full stop. This trips up business owners more than most, because they're used to thinking in billable hours and opportunity cost. The IRS doesn't work that way for volunteer labor, and no amount of documentation changes it.

What changes the math is everything you spend while doing that volunteer work. Those unreimbursed costs are where the actual deduction lives.

## What unreimbursed expenses qualify for a deduction

Per [IRS Publication 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526), you can deduct out-of-pocket expenses you pay to do volunteer work for a qualified organization, as long as the organization didn't reimburse you and the expenses are directly connected to the volunteer service. Common examples for people running e-commerce businesses on the side of nonprofit involvement include:

- Supplies you buy and donate for a charity event, like packing materials or printer paper for a fundraiser mailing
- Uniforms required by the organization that aren't suitable for everyday wear
- Phone or internet costs directly tied to volunteer coordination, if you can isolate the business-use portion
- Travel expenses for an out-of-town volunteer trip, including lodging and 50 percent of meals, as long as there's no significant element of personal vacation

What doesn't qualify is just as important. The value of lost income while you volunteer instead of fulfilling Shopify orders isn't deductible. Neither is the cost of childcare so you can volunteer, or the value of a discount you gave a nonprofit client on your own product. The line the IRS draws is cash you actually spent, not income you didn't earn.

## The charitable mileage deduction most volunteers miss

If you drove to volunteer, you have a deduction sitting in your mileage log that most people never claim. The charitable mileage rate is fixed by statute at 14 cents per mile, well below the business mileage rate, but it adds up faster than owners expect. A board member driving from Newtown Square to a quarterly nonprofit meeting in Philadelphia, roughly 16 miles each way, racks up nearly 100 miles a month just in commuting to service. Add trips to drop off donated returns or damaged inventory at a shelter, and a seller who thinks they have no deductible volunteer costs often has $150 to $300 a year sitting unclaimed.

You can also deduct parking fees and tolls incurred during volunteer driving, on top of the mileage. Keep a simple log: date, destination, purpose, and miles. That's the same discipline we recommend for [business mileage tracking when weighing a lease versus buying a vehicle](/resources/auto-leasing-vs-buying-which-makes-more-sense-2), and it holds up the same way if the IRS ever asks.

## Donating inventory instead of cash has its own rules

This is the one that catches multi-channel sellers off guard. If you donate unsold inventory (customer returns, discontinued SKUs, damaged-but-usable stock) to a qualified charity instead of writing it off as trash, the deduction isn't simply what you paid for it. For most sellers operating as a sole proprietor or pass-through entity, the deduction is generally limited to your cost basis in the inventory, not the retail price you would have charged on Amazon or Shopify. C corporations have access to an enhanced deduction under Section 170(e)(3) in certain cases, which is a conversation worth having with your CPA before you assume the bigger number applies to you.

Either way, the organization has to be a qualified 501(c)(3), and once the value of a single donation exceeds $250, you need a written acknowledgment from the charity. Cross $5,000 in donated goods and the IRS wants a qualified appraisal, not just your own estimate of value. This is a case where the paperwork requirement scales with the size of the gift, and skipping it is the single most common reason inventory donation deductions get denied on audit.

## What the IRS wants documented before you claim it

Most disallowed volunteer deductions don't fail because the expense wasn't legitimate. They fail because nobody kept proof. For any single contribution of $250 or more, whether it's cash, mileage, or a batch of donated inventory, you need a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the organization confirming the date, amount, and whether you received anything in return. For smaller out-of-pocket costs, a bank or credit card statement paired with a note about the volunteer purpose is usually enough.

Our advice to clients who serve on nonprofit boards or volunteer with organizations they also sell product through: keep volunteer expenses in a separate folder from business expenses, even though both live in your bookkeeping system. Mixing the two is how a legitimate charitable deduction turns into an argument with an IRS agent about whether it was really a marketing expense. If you're already dealing with commingled personal and business accounts, that's a pattern we see often enough in family office and multi-entity situations that it's worth fixing before tax season, not during it.

## Choosing a qualified organization matters

None of this works unless the organization you're giving time or goods to is a qualified organization under IRS rules. Most 501(c)(3) public charities qualify, but political organizations, most foreign charities, and individuals in need (even sympathetic ones) generally don't, no matter how good the cause. The [IRS Charities & Nonprofits Resource Hub](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits) includes a searchable tool to confirm an organization's status before you count on a deduction that won't hold up.

"We see this most with e-commerce owners who've built genuine relationships with local nonprofits over years of giving back," says Andrew C. Berg, CPA and founder of Berg Advisors. "The intent is always right. The problem is nobody told them the acknowledgment letter and the mileage log are what actually make the deduction stick."

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct the value of my time if I volunteer as a CPA, designer, or consultant?**
No. The IRS specifically disallows a deduction for the value of services you donate, regardless of your professional hourly rate. You can only deduct unreimbursed out-of-pocket expenses connected to that volunteer work, such as mileage, supplies, or required travel costs.

**What's the current charitable mileage deduction rate?**
The rate is fixed by statute at 14 cents per mile for miles driven in service of a qualified charitable organization, separate and lower than the standard business mileage rate. You can add parking fees and tolls on top of the mileage deduction, but you'll need a written log to support the claim.

**Do I need a receipt for every volunteer expense I want to deduct?**
For any single contribution of $250 or more, including inventory or a batch of related expenses, you need a written acknowledgment from the organization. Smaller expenses can usually be supported with a bank or credit card statement noting the volunteer purpose.

**Can I deduct inventory I donate from my e-commerce business?**
Generally yes, but for most pass-through entities the deduction is limited to your cost basis, not the retail price. Donations over $5,000 in value require a qualified appraisal, and C corporations may have access to an enhanced deduction under Section 170(e)(3) worth reviewing with your CPA.
