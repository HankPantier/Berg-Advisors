A $6,000 emergency room visit doesn't ask whether your Q4 inventory order just cleared. For a lot of the multi-channel sellers we work with in Newtown Square and beyond, that's exactly the collision that happens: a health crisis lands in the same month as a Prime Day restock or a slow payout cycle from Amazon or Walmart, and suddenly cash that was earmarked for product is covering an ambulance bill instead.

Most advice on medical costs stops at "build an emergency fund." That's not wrong, it's just incomplete. A cash cushion buys you time, but it doesn't reduce what you actually owe. [The tools that do that are tax-advantaged](/resources/why-tax-advantaged-planning-is-the-real-defense-against-medi-2): Health Savings Accounts, Flexible Spending Accounts, and the itemized medical expense deduction. Used together and set up before the crisis hits, they can turn a five-figure medical bill into something your business and your household can absorb without derailing next quarter's growth plans.

## Why an emergency fund alone falls short

The Federal Reserve's [Report on the Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/report-economic-well-being-us-households.htm) has tracked, year after year, how thin the margin is for a large share of American households when an unexpected expense hits. Savings help you survive the timing gap between the bill arriving and the insurance settling. They do nothing to change the underlying tax treatment of that spending.

Here's the distinction that matters for e-commerce owners specifically: your income is lumpy. A seller doing $1.5 million across Amazon, Shopify, and Etsy might see 40% of annual revenue land in November and December. If a medical event hits in March, before that cash arrives, an emergency fund is the only thing standing between you and a business credit card at 24% APR. But if you've built HSA and FSA contributions into your plan year-round, part of that bill was never fully taxed to begin with, and part of it may come back to you as a deduction. The fund covers timing. The tax strategy covers the actual cost.

## The tax-advantaged toolkit that blunts the damage

Three tools do the heavy lifting, and they work differently enough that most business owners are only using one when they could be using all three.

**Health Savings Accounts.** If you're on a qualifying high-deductible health plan, an HSA lets you contribute pre-tax dollars, grow them tax-free, and withdraw them tax-free for qualified medical expenses. For 2025, the IRS allows contributions up to $4,300 for self-only coverage and $8,550 for family coverage, with an extra $1,000 catch-up if you're 55 or older. Unlike an FSA, HSA balances roll over indefinitely and stay invested, which makes this the closest thing to a dedicated medical emergency fund that also lowers your tax bill.

**Flexible Spending Accounts.** FSAs also use pre-tax payroll dollars, but with a use-it-or-lose-it structure (some plans allow a small carryover or grace period). For an S-corp owner running payroll through the business, this is a straightforward way to pre-fund predictable costs like orthodontia, prescriptions, or planned procedures, and reduce payroll tax exposure at the same time.

**The itemized medical expense deduction.** This is the one most sellers forget about until a crisis forces the conversation. Under [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502), you can deduct unreimbursed medical expenses that exceed 7.5% of your adjusted gross income, but only if you itemize rather than take the standard deduction. For a household with $200,000 in AGI, that means the first $15,000 in medical costs isn't deductible, but everything past that is. A serious diagnosis, a surgery, or an extended hospital stay can clear that threshold fast, and timing which tax year those expenses land in (bunching elective procedures into one year, for example) can materially change your outcome.

## How seasonal e-commerce income changes the math

This is where generic personal finance advice breaks down for our clients. A W-2 employee has a predictable AGI and can plan HSA and FSA contributions against a steady paycheck. A multi-channel seller's AGI might swing by six figures year to year depending on inventory write-offs, ad spend, or a platform algorithm change that tanks Q3 sales.

That volatility actually works in your favor if you plan for it. In a strong year, front-loading HSA contributions and timing elective medical spending to clear the 7.5% AGI threshold can meaningfully lower your effective tax rate. In a lean year, the same medical expenses might not clear that threshold at all, which is exactly why the HSA (tax-free regardless of AGI) matters more than the itemized deduction in a down year. A CPA who understands your revenue pattern, not just your tax return, is the difference between guessing and planning.

Andrew Berg, CPA, who works with inventory-heavy e-commerce and CPG businesses on outsourced CFO support, puts it this way: the sellers who get burned aren't the ones with bad insurance. They're the ones who never modeled what a medical event does to a business that already runs tight on cash during restock season.

## Comparing the three tools

| Tool | Tax benefit | Best for | Key limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| HSA | Pre-tax in, tax-free growth, tax-free out | Ongoing cushion, rolls over year to year | $4,300 self-only / $8,550 family (2025) |
| FSA | Pre-tax payroll deduction | Predictable, planned expenses | Mostly use-it-or-lose-it |
| Itemized deduction | Deduct amounts above 7.5% of AGI | Large, unplanned medical events | Only helps if you itemize |

## Building a plan before the crisis, not during it

The sequence matters. Open and fund the HSA before the health event, not after, since contributions can't be made retroactively for expenses already incurred. Keep every receipt and Explanation of Benefits organized by tax year, because the itemized deduction only helps if you can substantiate the total. And if your bookkeeping is still on a cash basis, talk to your CPA about how a medical-related cash crunch shows up differently on accrual financials, particularly if you're carrying inventory financing or a line of credit that a bank is watching.

This is also where a broader look at your cash position pays off. If subscription costs or underused software tools are quietly draining a few hundred dollars a month, that's money that could be funding an HSA instead. We've written before about how [subscription creep](/resources/take-back-control-of-subscription-creep) adds up for sellers who never audit their recurring charges, and the same discipline applies to your emergency reserves. If your reserve cash is sitting in a brokerage account, it's also worth revisiting how that money is [allocated across your portfolio](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it-2), since money you might need in six months shouldn't be exposed the same way as your ten-year retirement savings. And if you're weighing whether to put a large medical bill on a rewards card while insurance sorts itself out, understand [how those rewards are actually taxed](/resources/business-credit-card-rewards-a-financial-strategy-for-busine-2) before you assume the points offset the interest.

We build this kind of planning into the quarterly advisory conversations we have with e-commerce clients, because a medical event shouldn't be the first time you're thinking about your AGI, your HSA balance, or your deduction threshold. It should already be part of the plan.

## Common Questions

**Can I use my HSA for a family member's medical bills, not just my own?**
Yes. HSA funds can cover qualified medical expenses for your spouse and any dependents you claim on your tax return, even if they aren't covered under your high-deductible health plan. Keep documentation showing the expense and the relationship, since that's what an auditor would ask for first.

**How much should an e-commerce seller keep in a medical emergency fund versus an HSA?**
A reasonable target is three to six months of personal living expenses in liquid savings, separate from your HSA. The HSA should be funded to the annual IRS limit if cash flow allows, since it's the only account offering both immediate tax savings and tax-free growth for future medical costs.

**Does the 7.5% AGI threshold reset every year?**
Yes, it applies to each tax year individually based on that year's adjusted gross income. This is why timing elective procedures, and knowing whether this year is a high-income or low-income year for your business, changes whether the itemized deduction is worth pursuing.

**What happens to unused FSA funds at year-end?**
Most plans require you to forfeit unused FSA balances, though some allow a grace period of up to 2.5 months or a limited carryover (check your specific plan document). Because of this, FSAs work best for predictable, budgeted expenses rather than as a general medical reserve.
