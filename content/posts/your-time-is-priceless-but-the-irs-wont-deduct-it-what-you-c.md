A lot of the e-commerce sellers we work with sit on nonprofit boards, run donation drives through their storefronts, or spend a Saturday a month [volunteering at a local food bank](/resources/your-time-is-priceless-but-the-irs-wont-deduct-it-what-you-c-2) in Delaware County. Come tax season, almost all of them ask the same question: can I deduct my time? The answer is no, and it surprises people every year. What does count is often bigger than they realize, and most of it goes untracked because nobody told them the rules.

The IRS treats your labor as worthless for tax purposes, no matter what your hourly rate would be if you billed for it. A graphic designer who spends 20 hours redesigning a nonprofit's logo can't deduct a dollar of that time, even at a market rate of $150 an hour. But the mileage she drove to meetings, the printer ink she bought for flyers, and the parking she paid downtown are often fully deductible. That gap between what people assume and what's actually true is where most of the missed deductions live.

## Your hours have no dollar value, but your wallet does

IRS Publication 526 is direct about this: you cannot deduct the value of your time or services, period, regardless of your profession or what you'd normally charge for the work. This trips up business owners more than most, because they're used to thinking in billable hours. A CPA can't deduct the value of pro bono tax prep. A contractor can't deduct the fair market value of labor donated to build a Habitat for Humanity house. That instinct to assign a dollar figure to your time needs to be set aside entirely when you're filing.

What does survive the IRS's scrutiny is unreimbursed, out-of-pocket spending directly connected to your volunteer work for a qualified 501(c)(3) organization. That distinction, between your labor and your cash outlay, is the entire concept this deduction rests on.

## What actually qualifies for a deduction

Here's where the math starts working in your favor, especially if you're active with a nonprofit board or run seasonal giving campaigns tied to your online store.

| Expense type | Deductible? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Volunteer hours or professional services | No | Never deductible, regardless of your normal rate |
| Mileage driving to/from volunteer work | Yes | 14 cents per mile (charitable rate), or actual gas and oil costs |
| Parking and tolls for volunteer trips | Yes | Must be tied directly to the volunteer activity |
| Supplies you buy and donate | Yes | Uniforms, event materials, food for a shelter, packaging for a donation drive |
| Travel (airfare, hotel) for a volunteer trip | Yes, with limits | No significant personal vacation element allowed |
| Meals while volunteering locally | Usually no | Only deductible if away from home overnight on nonprofit business |
| Value of donated inventory or product | Yes, at cost basis | Common for e-commerce sellers donating unsold stock |

The charitable mileage rate is fixed by statute at 14 cents per mile, far below the standard business mileage rate, so drivers sometimes decide it's not worth tracking. Over a year of weekly volunteer shifts, though, it adds up. A 15-mile round trip to a food bank every Saturday comes to roughly 780 miles a year, which is about $109 in deductible mileage alone, before you count parking or supplies.

For e-commerce sellers specifically, one deduction gets overlooked constantly: donated inventory. If you're clearing out excess stock at year-end and give it to a qualified charity instead of liquidating it at a loss, you can typically deduct the cost basis of that inventory rather than the retail price. That's a meaningfully different calculation than a straight charitable cash gift, and it's one more reason your books need to reflect inventory value accurately going into Q4. If your inventory accounting isn't already accrual-based and reconciled across Amazon, Shopify, and Walmart, this kind of deduction is easy to get wrong or miss entirely.

## The records the IRS actually wants to see

Unreimbursed volunteer expenses under $250 need a reasonably reliable record: a mileage log, a receipt, a bank statement showing the purchase. Once a single volunteer-related contribution crosses $250, the IRS requires a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the organization, dated before you file, confirming what you gave and that you received nothing of value in return. This is the same substantiation standard IRS Publication 526 lays out for any charitable contribution, and it's the rule people miss most often because they assume a canceled check is enough.

We tell clients to keep a simple running log, even a spreadsheet, noting the date, organization, purpose of the trip, and miles driven. Attach digital photos of receipts as you go. Trying to reconstruct four months of Saturday volunteer trips in April, from memory, rarely produces numbers an auditor will accept.

## Where this connects to your bigger tax picture

Volunteer deductions are itemized deductions, which means they only help if your total itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction. For 2024, that's $14,600 for single filers and $29,200 for married couples filing jointly. If you're already itemizing because of mortgage interest, state taxes, or larger charitable gifts, tracking these smaller volunteer costs is worth the effort. If you're taking the standard deduction most years, the math changes, and it's worth a real conversation about whether bunching charitable giving into alternating years makes more sense for your household.

This is the kind of question that gets lost between a big January inventory reconciliation and a February sales tax filing, which is exactly why we build quarterly advisory conversations into how we work with clients rather than waiting until March to talk taxes. If you're also thinking about how nonprofit board work or donation strategy intersects with other financial decisions, like [balancing your investment portfolio](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it-2) or managing [subscription creep](/resources/take-back-control-of-subscription-creep) that's quietly draining margin you could otherwise give away, those conversations tend to happen together.

The IRS Charities & Nonprofits hub is also worth a look if you're on a board and want to confirm an organization's exempt status before you donate inventory or cash. You can find it at the [IRS Charities & Nonprofits Resource Hub](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits).

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct the value of professional services I donate to a nonprofit?**
No. The IRS explicitly disallows deducting the fair market value of your time or professional services, even if you'd normally bill $200 an hour for the same work. You can only deduct actual out-of-pocket costs tied to providing that service, like materials or mileage.

**What's the mileage rate for volunteer driving in 2024?**
The charitable mileage rate is 14 cents per mile, set by statute rather than adjusted annually like the business rate. You can also deduct actual gas and oil costs instead of using the flat rate, but you can't combine both methods for the same trip.

**Do I need a receipt for every volunteer expense?**
For any single contribution under $250, a reliable record like a mileage log or receipt is enough. Once a contribution reaches $250 or more, IRS Publication 526 requires a written acknowledgment from the organization before you file.

**Can I deduct inventory I donate from my e-commerce business?**
Generally yes, at the inventory's cost basis rather than its retail price. This is a common year-end move for sellers clearing slow-moving SKUs, but it needs to line up with how your books value that inventory, which is where accrual accounting matters.
